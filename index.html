The Meaning and Nature of Philosophy

What is Philosophy?

The word Philosophy comes from two Greek words: Philo (love) and Sophia (wisdom). Together they mean "love of wisdom" or "love of knowledge." It is a strong desire to uncover what is hidden — an enquiry into the unknown for better understanding.

Simple Definition: Philosophy is the systematic, rational, and critical investigation of fundamental questions about existence, knowledge, values, and reality.

Key Thinkers and Their Views

  • Carter Good: Philosophy is a science that organises and systematises all fields of knowledge — comprising logic, ethics, aesthetics, metaphysics, and epistemology.
  • Russell (2010): Philosophy seeks to examine assumptions underlying human thought and action, enabling individuals to think clearly and rationally.
  • Jalaluddin & Idi (2013): Philosophy is a comprehensive and systematic view of the entire domain of knowledge.
  • Stanford Encyclopedia: Philosophy fosters critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and conceptual clarity.

Characteristics of Philosophy

  • Rational and Critical – Proceeds through argumentation and logical evaluation, not mere opinion.
  • Reflective and Second-order – Reflects on the foundations and methods of other disciplines.
  • Systematic and Coherent – Organises ideas into logically consistent systems, avoiding contradiction.
  • Speculative yet Grounded – Ventures beyond observation to address ultimate questions, while anchored in reason.
  • Normative and Evaluative – Concerned with what ought to be, not just what is.
  • Universal and Foundational – Addresses questions that transcend cultures (truth, knowledge, existence).
  • Dialectical – Progresses through dialogue, debate, and confrontation of opposing views.
  • Practical and Transformative – Shapes human life, guiding action and moral decision-making.

Why Philosophy Matters

Philosophy shapes human self-understanding, ethical norms, cultural values, education, and political structures. It asks fundamental questions: What is knowledge? How can we know that we know? What are the limits of human knowledge?

It involves systematic thinking — coherent reasoning about the whole domain of our experience, using logic, language analysis, and critical analysis.

Modes of Philosophy

What are Modes of Philosophy?

Modes of philosophy are the various approaches or methods through which philosophical thinking takes place. There are four main modes: Speculative, Prescriptive, Analytic, and Dialectical.

1. Speculative Mode

This is thinking systematically and critically about everything that exists, seeking to understand how all experience forms a meaningful whole. It is a search for wholeness and order of knowledge. Key words: what, why, who, where.

Key feature: Concerned with ultimate reality and existence — asks about being, God, the soul, causality, and the nature of the universe. Goes beyond empirical observation. Associated with Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas.

2. Prescriptive Mode

This mode is interested in establishing standards for assessing values, judging conduct, and appraising art. It asks: What is good or bad? Right or wrong? Beautiful or ugly?

Key feature: Normative — deals with what ought to be rather than what is. Evaluates human actions, institutions, and ideals. Associated with Plato (justice and the good life).

3. Analytic Mode

This mode is interested in words and meaning. It shows how inconsistencies arise when meanings used in one context are applied in another. The task is to clarify language and concepts by exposing their inconsistencies.

Key feature: Focus on clarity, precision, and logical structure. Associated with Bertrand Russell, who emphasized logical analysis as the proper method.

4. Dialectical Mode

This mode involves dialogue, argument, and synthesis of opposing viewpoints. Truth emerges through the interaction of thesis and antithesis. It reflects philosophy as a dynamic and evolving discipline.

Key feature: Rooted in the tradition of Socrates, who used questioning and dialogue to uncover deeper insights.

Relevance of the Modes

  • They make philosophy comprehensive — addressing reality, knowledge, and values from different angles (Aristotle).
  • They ensure clarity and logical rigour — especially through analysis (Bertrand Russell).
  • They promote critical thinking — questioning assumptions, avoiding dogmatism (Socrates).
  • They provide moral and practical guidance — linking philosophy to conduct (Thomas Aquinas).
  • They make philosophy dynamic and progressive — growing through dialogue and critique.

Branches of Philosophy

The Four Major Branches

The major branches are: Metaphysics, Epistemology, Logic, and Axiology. They are also called the problems, structures, or components of philosophy.

1. Metaphysics

From Greek: Meta (after/beyond) + Physika (nature). Metaphysics seeks to explain elements of reality not easily discovered through everyday experience. It studies ultimate and fundamental reality — the nature of God, the universe, man, and life.

Sub-branches: Ontology (study of being/existence), Cosmology (origin/development of the universe), Causality (cause and effect), Theology (conceptions of God).
  • Ontology: "Science of being." Asks what it means for anything to exist. Is basic reality matter, spirit, or both? Is reality fixed or always changing?
  • Cosmology: How did the universe originate? Did it come by accident or design?
  • Theology: Is there a God? What are God's attributes? Why does evil exist if God is all-good and all-powerful?

2. Epistemology

From Greek: Episteme (knowledge) + Logos (study of). Epistemology is the theory of knowledge — studying its nature, source, and validity. Key question: "What is true?" and "How do we know that we know?"

Key issues: Skepticism (can we know reality?), Absolute vs. Relative truth, Sources of knowledge.

Five Sources of Knowledge:

  • Revelation – Knowledge from God/deity. Accepted by faith; cannot be empirically proved or disproved.
  • Empirical – Through the five senses: hearing, seeing, feeling, smelling, tasting. Open to replication.
  • Authoritative – From experts, tradition, textbooks, teachers. Danger: if built on incorrect assumptions, knowledge is distorted.
  • Rational – Through reasoning faculty. Rationalists believe the mind can arrive at knowledge independently of the senses. Uses formal logic.
  • Intuition – Comes suddenly and directly without reasoning. Common in philosophy and music. Weakness: not safe when used alone.

3. Axiology

From Greek: Axios (worth/value) + Logos (theory). Axiology is the study of judgements about values. It includes both ethics and aesthetics.

  • Ethics: Study of what makes actions right or wrong. Includes meta-ethics, value theory, theory of conduct, and applied ethics. Key question: "How should I behave?"
  • Aesthetics: Study of what is beautiful, enjoyable, or tasteful. "What is beautiful?" and "What should I like?" Governs the creation and appreciation of beauty and art.

4. Logic

The branch concerned with reasoning and the distinction between good and bad reasoning. It involves methods and principles for determining the correctness of an argument.

  • Deductive Logic: From general to specific (universal to particular). Conclusion follows necessarily from premises. Uses syllogism (major premise, minor premise, conclusion).
  • Inductive Logic: From specific to general (particular to universal). Conclusion is probable but not guaranteed.

TOPIC 4

Syllogism – Elements, Types, and Rules

What is a Syllogism?

A syllogism is a three-step deductive reasoning method: Major Premise (assumed fact), Minor Premise (supports the major premise), and Conclusion (drawn from both premises). It is a tool to establish logical truth.

Classic example: All mammals are warm-blooded (Major) / All dogs are mammals (Minor) / Therefore, all dogs are warm-blooded (Conclusion). The shared term "mammals" connects the premises.

Types of Syllogism

  • Conditional Syllogism: "If…then" structure. E.g., If today is sunny → the race will be held → Today is sunny → the race will be held.
  • Categorical Syllogism: States categories directly. E.g., No reptiles are warm-blooded / Some pets are reptiles / Therefore, some pets are not warm.
  • Disjunctive Syllogism: "Either/or" structure — if one option is ruled out, the other must be true.
  • Hypothetical Syllogism: Chain of conditionals. If A→B and B→C, then A→C.

Rules of Syllogism

  • If the major premise is affirmative, the conclusion must be affirmative.
  • If the minor premise is negative, the conclusion must be negative.
  • The conclusion should combine the unique elements of the major and minor premises.

Figures of Syllogism

1st Figure: Subject is in major, predicate in minor. 2nd Figure: Predicate of conclusion appears in both premises. 3rd Figure: Subject of conclusion is predicate of both premises. 4th Figure: Predicate of major = subject of minor.

Illicit Major

An illicit major is an invalid conclusion where a term used in the conclusion was not properly distributed in the major premise. Example: "All dogs love food; John loves food; therefore John is a dog" — INVALID.

TOPIC 5

Symbolic Logic & Rules of Inference

Summary Notes

Symbolic logic uses premises (facts given) to reach a conclusion through legal, step-by-step moves. There are 9 Rules of Inference:

  • 1. Modus Ponens (M.P.) – If P→Q and P is true, then Q is true. (Affirming the antecedent)
  • 2. Modus Tollens (M.T.) – If P→Q and Q is false, then P is false. (Denying the consequent)
  • 3. Hypothetical Syllogism (H.S.) – If P→Q and Q→R, then P→R. (Chain rule)
  • 4. Disjunctive Syllogism (D.S.) – If P∨Q and ¬P, then Q. (Eliminating a false option)
  • 5. Constructive Dilemma (C.D.) – Combines two conditionals with a disjunction
  • 6. Absorption (Abs.) – If P→Q, then P→(P∧Q). (Bundles cause and effect)
  • 7. Simplification (Simp.) – If P∧Q, then P (or Q). (Take one from a "both…and")
  • 8. Conjunction (Conj.) – If P is true and Q is true separately, then P∧Q.
  • 9. Addition (Add.) – If P is true, then P∨Q is true (attaching "or" to a truth).
Key distinction: "True" refers to the content of statements. "Valid" refers to whether the conclusion follows logically from the premises. A valid argument can have false premises and even a false conclusion.

Common errors: Converse Error – mistaking Q→P for P→Q. Inverse Error – concluding ¬Q from ¬P when only P→Q is known.
